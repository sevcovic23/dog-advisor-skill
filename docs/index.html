Dog Care Basics

A practical worksheet for understanding your dog, solving common problems, and building a relationship based on trust — not force.

Foundation

How dogs actually learn

Every behaviour your dog does is shaped by what happens after it. There are four basic ways behaviour is reinforced or reduced. Understanding these helps you train smarter.

🎁

Add something good → behaviour increases

You give a treat, toy, or praise the moment the dog does something right. This is positive reinforcement — your most powerful tool. Use it constantly.

Example: dog sits → treat. Dog walks calmly → marker + treat.

😮‍💨

Remove something uncomfortable → behaviour increases

Leash pressure releases the moment the dog steps into position. The relief reinforces the behaviour. This is negative reinforcement — a skilled technique when used gently.

Example: gentle leash tension → dog moves closer → tension releases → dog learns to stay close.

⏸️

Remove something good → behaviour decreases

Walk away, withdraw attention, or end the game the moment the dog does something you don't want. No drama. The dog learns this behaviour leads nowhere.

Example: dog jumps → you turn away. Dog bites too hard → you stop playing.

🚫

Add something unpleasant → avoid this

Shock collars, choke chains used painfully, hitting. This may stop a behaviour short-term but damages trust, creates anxiety, and causes fallout behaviours. Avoid.

Communication

The marker system

Dogs live in the present. A treat that arrives 3 seconds after a good behaviour might reward whatever happened in between. A marker — a click or a short word — bridges the gap. It tells the dog the exact moment they got it right.

The sequence — always in this order

Give cue
→
Dog responds
→
Marker
"yes" / click
→
Reward arrives

Choose one marker and stick to it. A clicker is precise. A short spoken word ("yes", "good", "šik") works just as well and you always have it with you. Once the marker sounds, the reward always follows — this is a promise to your dog. Break it and the marker loses meaning.

Daily Routine

Daily care worksheet

Click each item to mark it done. A well-cared-for dog has physical needs, mental needs, and clear communication met every day.

  • Morning exerciseAt least 30–45 minutes of real physical activity — not just a garden wander. Sniff-heavy walks count double: letting your dog follow their nose is mentally tiring. Tired dog = calmer dog.
  • Earn breakfast (not from a bowl)Give 3/4 of the daily food through training, games, or scatter feeding — not just a bowl left on the floor. Dogs that work for food have better focus and more motivation to train. If they ignore the bowl, pick it up after 2 minutes.
  • Short training session (5–10 minutes)Short and sharp beats long and boring. Practice one or two things. Use your marker. End on a success. Twice a day is ideal — morning and evening.
  • Self-control exercise3–5 repetitions of the activation–calming cycle (see below). Builds emotional regulation that transfers to every other situation.
  • Mental stimulationA sniff walk, a frozen Kong, a puzzle feeder, a search game (hide treats around the house), or a shaping session with a new trick. 10 minutes of nose work is equivalent to 30 minutes of physical walking.
  • Calm, structured rest timeDogs need 14–16 hours of sleep. Practice "place" or "mat" — sending your dog to a settled spot. Rest is not passive; teaching a dog to switch off is a skill.
  • One rewarded recall practiceCall your dog once, they come, they get the best treat you have. Then release them. Do this every single day. Recall is a habit — let it decay and it will fail exactly when you need it.
Problem Solving

Common issues — quick reference

Every issue has two parts: management (what to do today to stop it getting worse) and training (the actual fix over weeks). You need both.

📢

Barking alone

Usually separation anxiety — the dog hasn't learned that being alone is safe and temporary. Often triggered by a routine change.

  • Keep arrivals and departures completely low-key
  • Practice short absences — 30 seconds, then 2 minutes, then 5 — on weekends too
  • Frozen stuffed Kong given only when you leave (freeze overnight)
  • Consider a midday visit to break up the time alone
Common mistakeMaking a big fuss when leaving or arriving — it signals to the dog that your departure is a major event worth getting anxious about.
🦷

Puppy biting

Normal at 3–6 months. Puppies explore with their mouths. They need to learn that human skin has a very low bite threshold.

  • Freeze the moment teeth touch skin — do not pull away
  • After 2 seconds, calmly stand up, fold arms, turn away for 30 seconds
  • Always have a toy in hand before play starts
  • Stop all movement when they bite ankles — movement is the trigger
Common mistakePulling your hand away — this looks like fleeing prey and makes the game more exciting, not less.
🐕

Pulling on the leash

The dog has learned that pulling gets them forward. Every step you took while being pulled rewarded the pulling.

  • Stop completely every time the leash goes tight — do not move forward
  • Reward calm walking at your hip, not in front
  • Start indoors, then a quiet street, then busier environments
  • Harness = free to sniff. Training collar = walking beside me
Common mistakeRewarding the dog in front of your body — this teaches them to be ahead of you, which is exactly the position pulling produces.
😤

Reactivity to other dogs

The dog is frustrated, fearful, or has learned that barking makes other dogs go away. The emotional response is now automatic.

  • No unplanned greetings with unknown dogs
  • Use your body as a barrier between your dog and the trigger
  • Find the distance where they notice but don't react — train there
  • Mark and reward calm looking at the other dog (Look at That technique)
Common mistakeLetting the dog greet after reacting — the greeting rewards the reactivity.
🏃

Not coming when called

Coming to you has been associated with the end of fun, or the cue has been repeated so many times without response that it has lost meaning.

  • Never call unless you are confident they will come — go to them instead
  • When you call, run away from the dog — dogs chase movement
  • Recall always predicts the best reward you have
  • Practice multiple short recalls per walk, then release back to play
Common mistakeCalling the dog to tell them off or to end the walk — this poisons the recall cue faster than anything else.
🙋

Jumping on people

At some point jumping produced attention — even being pushed away counts as interaction. The dog learned it works.

  • The moment the dog jumps, all attention disappears — turn away, no words, no eye contact
  • The moment four paws are on the floor, give the attention they wanted
  • Ask for a sit before greeting — a dog that is sitting cannot jump
  • Every person the dog meets must follow the same rule
Common mistakeSaying "no" or "down" — any word is attention, and attention is the reward the dog is jumping for.
Core Exercise

The self-control exercise

Many behaviour problems — reactivity, jumping, biting, pulling — come from a dog that cannot regulate its own emotions when excited. This exercise builds that capacity and transfers across all situations. Do it daily.

The goal is to teach your dog to flip between aroused and calm on cue. A dog that can do this is much easier to work with everywhere — on the street, around other dogs, at the vet.

1

Wild play — 5 to 10 seconds

Tug, chase a toy, throw a ball — whatever your dog finds exciting. Really get them going. Short bursts are better than long sessions.

2

Stop and cue calm — "drop it", "enough"

End the play with a clear cue. Put the toy away or behind your back. The dog should not still have access to it.

3

Ask for a down-stay — 5 to 10 seconds

Wait for calm. If the dog holds the down for the full time, marker + immediately back to play. If they break early, use your error signal ("nope"), guide them back, and make the next repetition shorter.

4

Back to wild play

The play is the reward for the calm. Repeat 2–4 times per session. End on a success.

Reading Your Dog

Stress and arousal levels

Training only works in the middle zone. When a dog is over threshold — highly reactive, panicked, or extremely excited — their hearing shuts down first. Talking to them does not help.

What to look for

🟢 Calm — train here
  • Loose, relaxed body
  • Soft eyes, relaxed mouth
  • Normal breathing
  • Takes treats easily
  • Responds to cues
🟡 Alert — proceed carefully
  • Ears forward, tail up
  • Quick movements
  • Still takes treats
  • Can follow cues
  • Focused but manageable
🔴 Over threshold — manage only
  • Fixation, stiff body
  • Barking, lunging
  • Ignores treats
  • Cannot hear commands
  • Panting, yawning, shaking
When your dog is over threshold, stop talking. Use your body as a barrier. Use the leash as a calm, steady guide — not a yank. Increase distance from the trigger first. Then, once the dog is calm, address the problem through training later. Never try to train a dog that cannot hear you.
Remember

10 golden rules

1

Reward good attempts, not just perfect responses

Partial success is still progress. A dog moving in the right direction deserves acknowledgement, especially early in learning.

2

Start indoors, always

A dog that can do something indoors cannot necessarily do it outside. When the environment gets harder, drop your criteria back down to basics.

3

Never repeat a cue the dog has already ignored

If they didn't respond, the environment is too hard or they don't know the behaviour well enough yet. Repeating teaches them the first cue doesn't matter.

4

Management stops practice; training stops the problem

Every time a dog practises a bad behaviour, they get better at it. Use management to prevent practice while you work on training.

5

After an error, make the next task easier

Error signal → guide back to position → do not reward the return → make the next attempt simpler. End on a success.

6

The reward is whatever the dog actually values in that moment

Treats, toys, freedom to sniff, access to another dog. If the dog won't take food, they are too stressed. Address the arousal level first.

7

Consistency across all people in the household

One person allowing the dog to jump while another corrects it teaches the dog to jump at person A. Rules must be the same for everyone.

8

Short sessions beat long sessions

Five minutes twice a day beats forty minutes once a week. Dogs learn better in short, focused bursts. Always end before they get bored or frustrated.

9

What the dog figures out itself is stronger than what you lure them into

Shaping (rewarding small steps toward a behaviour) produces more reliable results than always guiding with a treat. Use luring to introduce new things; fade it quickly.

10

When in doubt, see a professional in person

Serious aggression, deep anxiety, and complex behaviour problems need eyes on the dog. A good trainer watches things no guide can catch.
