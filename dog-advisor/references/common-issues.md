# Common Issues — Causes and Training Approaches

Read this file when the user describes a specific problem. Use it alongside `training-principles.md`.

---

## Pulling on the leash

**Why it happens:** The dog has learned that pulling gets them where they want to go. Every time they pulled and you kept walking, the behaviour was rewarded by forward movement.

**Management now:** Stop completely every time the leash goes tight. Do not move forward until it is loose. Be consistent — even one step while they are pulling teaches them it sometimes works.

**Training approach:**
1. Start indoors or in a very quiet place with almost no distractions
2. Walk a few steps → the moment the dog moves into position beside you, marker + reward delivered to your hip (not in front of you — rewarding in front encourages the dog to be ahead of you)
3. Build gradually: a few steps → reward. More steps → reward. Only add distractions once this is reliable
4. Use the "ťuk ťuk" technique for corrections: a brief rhythmic leash signal (not a yank) to bring the dog back into position when they drift, followed immediately by a reward when they return
5. Create a clear distinction: harness = free to explore and pull, training collar = walk beside me

**Common mistakes:** Continuing to walk while the dog pulls (rewards pulling), rewarding in front of the body (encourages forging ahead), allowing the dog to greet other dogs while pulling (the greeting becomes the reward for pulling).

## Reactivity to other dogs (barking, lunging)

**Why it happens:** The dog is either frustrated (wants to greet but can't), fearful (feels threatened), or has learned that barking makes other dogs go away. In all cases, the emotional response has become automatic.

**Management now:**
- No unplanned greetings with unknown dogs
- Use your body as a barrier — step between your dog and the trigger
- Increase distance until the dog can notice the other dog without reacting (this is "under threshold")
- Stay calm and move like a leader — your tension travels down the leash

**Training approach (under threshold only — if the dog is already reacting, manage first):**
1. Find the distance at which your dog can notice another dog without reacting — this is your working distance
2. At that distance, ask for a simple known behaviour (nose touch, eye contact, sit) → marker → reward
3. This is the "Look at That" (LAT) technique: dog sees trigger → you mark and reward the dog for noticing calmly → dog learns that other dogs predict treats
4. Gradually decrease distance only as the dog shows consistent calm
5. Activation–calming cycles (see training-principles.md) build the dog's general ability to regulate emotions, which transfers to reactive situations

**Critical note on stress:** When the dog is already reacting (barking, lunging), verbal commands do not work — the dog cannot hear them. Do not repeat "no" or the dog's name. Use the leash calmly to increase distance and get out of the situation. Then address it through training later.

**Common mistakes:** Letting the dog greet after reacting (the greeting rewards the reactivity), pulling the leash tight when you see another dog (signals danger to the dog), repeating commands the dog is ignoring.

## Barking when left alone (separation anxiety)

**Why it happens:** The dog has not learned that being alone is safe and temporary. Often triggered or worsened by a change in routine (owner returning to office, house move, new baby).

**Management now:**
- A dog walker or midday visit breaks up the time alone and reduces the distress period
- Background noise (radio, TV) can reduce alertness to outside sounds
- Avoid dramatic departures and arrivals — keep them low-key and calm

**Training approach:**
1. Gradual departures: leave for 30 seconds, return. Then 2 minutes. Then 5. Build up slowly over days — the dog needs to learn that you always come back and it is no big deal
2. Create a positive pre-departure ritual: a specific phrase or action that signals "I'm leaving, and something good is coming" — a frozen stuffed Kong given only at departure time
3. The Kong should be frozen (peanut butter, kibble, banana, yoghurt) so it lasts 30–45 minutes instead of 5
4. Do practice departures on weekends too — 5–10 short ones builds the pattern faster
5. Do not punish the dog after barking — they cannot connect the punishment to the earlier behaviour, and it makes homecoming more stressful

**Common mistakes:** Making arrivals and departures emotional (increases the dog's anxiety about the event), only giving the high-value treat at real departures (the dog learns to dread it as a signal you're leaving for a long time), punishing after the fact.

## Puppy biting / mouthing

**Why it happens:** Normal behaviour at 3–6 months. Puppies explore with their mouths, and they learned from littermates how hard they can bite — but that lesson doesn't automatically transfer to humans.

**Management now:** Do not let children or elderly people interact with the puppy unsupervised during this phase.

**Training approach:**
1. Freeze — the moment teeth touch skin, stop moving completely. Do not pull away (this looks like fleeing prey and excites the dog more), do not yelp (many puppies find it exciting rather than aversive)
2. After freezing for 1–2 seconds, calmly end the interaction — stand up, fold arms, turn away for 30 seconds. Puppies hate being ignored more than almost anything
3. Before play starts, always have a toy in hand — redirect biting onto the toy, not your hands
4. For ankle biting: stop moving the moment it starts. Movement is the trigger
5. For puppies already from a litter: they have some bite inhibition from siblings. Build on it

**Common mistakes:** Pulling hand away (triggers prey drive), yelping (can excite rather than stop), pushing the dog away (becomes a game), inconsistency between family members (one person corrects, another plays rough).

**Timeline:** With consistency, significant improvement by 5–6 months for most breeds. Golden Retrievers and Labs often improve faster; terriers and herding breeds may take longer.

## Jumping on people

**Why it happens:** At some point, jumping was rewarded — with attention, eye contact, or being pushed down (which is still interaction). The dog learned that jumping gets a response.

**Management now:** Ask everyone in the household and visitors to follow the same rule immediately — otherwise training is undermined every day.

**Training approach:**
1. Negative Punishment (NT): the moment the dog jumps, all attention disappears — turn away, cross arms, no eye contact, no words. The moment four paws are on the floor, marker + reward
2. Ask for an incompatible behaviour: a dog that is sitting cannot jump. As soon as the dog sits, give the attention they were seeking. The dog learns that sitting produces what jumping used to produce
3. Practice with strangers: explain what to do before they approach. One inconsistent greeting undoes multiple training sessions

**Common mistakes:** Pushing the dog down (physical contact = reward), saying "no" or "down" (attention = reward), kneeing the dog (can hurt, creates distrust, and doesn't address the cause).

## Not coming when called (recall)

**Why it happens:** Coming to you has been associated with the end of fun (being put on lead, going home), or the owner has repeated the cue so many times without follow-through that it has lost meaning.

**Management now:** Do not call the dog unless you are confident they will come. If not, go to them. Every ignored recall weakens the cue.

**Training approach:**
1. Reload the recall cue in a low-distraction environment — call once, immediately run away from the dog (dogs chase movement), and when they arrive, give the best reward you have (high-value food, or the toy they love most)
2. The "restrained recall": one person holds the dog, the other runs away and calls enthusiastically. When the dog strains against the hold and is clearly eager to go, release them. The anticipation builds excitement
3. Recall should always predict something great — never call the dog to tell them off, to give them a bath, or to end the fun permanently. Call, reward, then send them back to play
4. Practice many short recalls during walks — call, reward, release back to sniffing. This way recall does not mean the walk is over

**Common mistakes:** Calling the dog to punish them for something they did earlier (poisons the recall cue), calling repeatedly when the dog ignores (teaches the dog that the first call doesn't matter), putting the lead on only when leaving (the dog learns that recall = end of fun).

## Pulling towards / excessive interest in other dogs

**Why it happens:** The dog has learned that straining forward eventually produces access to another dog. Or the dog has high social drive and no impulse control around the trigger.

**Training approach:**
1. The dog must learn that pulling never produces the desired outcome — calm, loose lead does
2. If the dog can remain calm at a distance, reward with access to the other dog as the reward (controlled greeting, brief, then move on)
3. Use self-control exercises daily (activation–calming cycles) to build the dog's general impulse control
4. Only allow greetings when the lead is loose — the moment tension appears, turn and walk away before greeting

## Not staying / breaking position (down-stay, sit-stay)

**Why it happens:** The criteria were raised too fast (too much distance, too long duration, too many distractions before the dog was ready), or the dog was rewarded after breaking position (accidentally teaching them that getting up is fine).

**Training approach:**
1. Go back to basics: one step away, return, marker while dog is still in position, reward delivered on the ground (to prevent the dog from getting up to reach it)
2. The marker is a "permission pass" — it tells the dog they can move after the reward. Say the marker only when you are back beside the dog and they are still in position
3. Build distance, duration, and distraction separately — not all at once
4. If the dog breaks position: use the error signal ("nope"), guide them back to the original spot (not a new spot), do not reward the return, make the next attempt easier

**Common mistakes:** Rewarding after the dog has already stood up (rewards breaking position), raising criteria too fast, using the dog's name as a recall when they break (teaches the dog that breaking position = getting called).
