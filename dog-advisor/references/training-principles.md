# Training Principles

This reference contains the core methodology to apply when giving advice. It is based on real training session notes and positive reinforcement methodology.

---

## The four quadrants of learning

All dog behaviour is shaped by consequences. When giving advice, reason through which quadrant applies:

- **Positive Reinforcement (PP)** — add something the dog wants (treat, toy, sniff, play) when the dog does something right. The behaviour becomes more likely. This is the primary tool.
- **Negative Reinforcement (NP)** — remove something the dog finds uncomfortable when they do the right thing (e.g. leash pressure releases the moment the dog steps into position). The behaviour becomes more likely.
- **Negative Punishment (NT)** — remove something the dog wants when they do the wrong thing (stop play when dog bites too hard, withdraw attention when dog jumps, withhold treat when dog pulls). The behaviour becomes less likely.
- **Positive Punishment (PT)** — add something unpleasant when the dog does the wrong thing. Avoid recommending this — it damages trust and creates fallout behaviours. Never recommend shock collars, choke chains used painfully, or physical corrections.

Most everyday problems are best solved with PP + NT. NP (pressure and release) is a skilled technique — mention it only when relevant and explain it clearly.

---

## The marker system

Timing is everything in dog training. A marker tells the dog the exact moment they did the right thing — even if the treat arrives a second later.

**How it works:**
- Choose a marker: a clicker, or a short spoken word (e.g. "yes", "good", "šik")
- The sequence is always: cue → dog does the right thing → marker → reward
- The marker must come at the exact moment of the correct behaviour, not after
- Once the marker sounds, the reward always follows — the contract must be kept

**When to mention it:**
- Always suggest a marker system if the owner doesn't already use one
- If they use treats but no marker, suggest adding one — it speeds up learning significantly
- The marker is especially important when the reward can't be delivered instantly

## Criteria building — start simple, add difficulty gradually

Never train the final behaviour from day one. Break it into small steps and only raise the difficulty once the dog succeeds reliably at the current level.

The order of difficulty is:
1. **Indoors, no distractions** — this is always step one
2. **Indoors with mild distractions** (another person in the room, a toy on the floor)
3. **Outdoors in a quiet place**
4. **Outdoors with distractions** (other dogs, people, traffic)

When the environment gets harder, drop the criteria back down. A dog that can walk calmly beside you indoors may need to start from scratch on a busy street — that is normal, not failure.

**Error handling:**
- When the dog makes a mistake in training, use a calm error signal ("nope", "nene", "uh-uh") — this is not punishment, it just means "that behaviour leads nowhere"
- After an error, make the task easier for the next attempt so the dog can succeed
- Never repeat a cue the dog has already ignored — if they didn't respond, the environment is too hard or the dog doesn't know the behaviour well enough yet
- Always reward good attempts, not just perfect responses — partial success is still progress

## Management vs training — always give both

**Management** = controlling the environment to prevent the problem from happening or getting worse right now. It does not teach the dog anything new, but it stops rehearsal of the bad behaviour (every time a dog practises a bad behaviour, it gets better at it).

Examples of management:
- Putting the dog on lead around other dogs until recall is reliable
- Using your body as a barrier when a reactive dog spots another dog
- Giving a long-line instead of off-lead until the dog is ready
- Not letting the dog greet every dog on the street during a reactivity rehabilitation programme

**Training** = the longer process of actually changing the behaviour through repetition, rewards, and criteria building.

Always give the owner something for management (today) and something for training (the weeks ahead).

## Self-control as a foundation skill

Many behaviour problems — reactivity, jumping, pulling, biting — are rooted in a dog that cannot regulate its own emotions when excited or frustrated. Self-control exercises build this capacity and transfer across situations.

**The activation–calming cycle:**
- Short burst of excitement (tug, chase, fetch — 5–10 seconds)
- Cue to stop ("drop it", "enough")
- Immediately ask for a calm behaviour (down stay for 5–10 seconds)
- Back to excitement
- Repeat

A dog that can flip between aroused and calm on cue is much easier to work with everywhere. Recommend this as a daily exercise for any dog that struggles with impulse control.

**The error during self-control:**
- If the dog breaks the calm behaviour (gets up from down before released), use the error signal, guide them back to position, do not reward the return — make the next repetition easier

## Reading stress and arousal

Dogs communicate their emotional state through body language. When advising, ask the owner to think about what the dog looks like in the moment:

- **Low arousal / calm**: loose body, relaxed mouth, normal breathing, interest without fixation
- **Medium arousal / engaged**: alert posture, focused, tail up, quick movement — this is the training zone
- **High arousal / over threshold**: fixation, barking, lunging, inability to take treats, ignoring commands — training is not possible here, only management

**Critical point — senses shut down under high stress:**
When a dog is over threshold (highly reactive, panicked, or extremely excited), hearing effectively shuts off first. Calling the dog's name or giving verbal commands does not work. The owner should:
- Stop talking
- Use the leash as a calm, steady communication tool
- Use their body to block, redirect, or increase distance
- Get the dog out of the situation first, then address it in training later

## Harness vs training collar

If the owner mentions equipment, give this guidance:
- **Harness** = freedom, exploration, sniffing walks — the dog knows this means relaxed time
- **Training collar** (flat collar, slip lead, or similar) = work mode — the dog learns to pay more attention when this is on
- Using them consistently creates a clear signal to the dog about what mode they are in
- Never use a training collar as everyday equipment — the distinction matters

---

## Rewards — what counts as a reward

Rewards are not just treats. Use what the dog actually finds valuable in the moment:

- **Food** — most reliable, easiest to control quantity and timing
- **Play/toy** — powerful for high-drive dogs, useful for building excitement
- **The environment** — permission to sniff, to run, to greet another dog. "Volno" (free) can be a reward if access to something the dog wants follows

When a dog won't take food, it is usually a sign of high stress, not a sign the training approach is wrong. Address the arousal level first.

---

## Food drive — building motivation

A dog that doesn't care about treats is hard to train. To build food motivation:
- The dog should earn most of its daily food through work and games — not just from a bowl
- Make mealtime active: scatter kibble, use it in games, ask for behaviours before meals
- If the dog ignores a bowl of food, pick it up after 2 minutes — hunger is a training tool
- High-value treats (chicken, cheese, sausage) for new or hard behaviours; regular kibble for well-known easy ones

---

## Shaping vs luring

**Luring** — using a treat to physically guide the dog into position. Fast to teach, but the dog can become dependent on seeing the treat.

**Shaping** — rewarding small steps toward the behaviour without guiding. The dog figures it out. Slower, but the behaviour is more reliable and generalises better.

Use luring to introduce a completely new behaviour. Fade the lure quickly (within 2–3 sessions). Use shaping to build behaviours the dog needs to problem-solve.

**Reset** — when shaping, throw a treat away from the dog after each attempt. This gets the dog moving again and creates a natural start position for the next rep. Prevents the dog from getting stuck and frustrated.
